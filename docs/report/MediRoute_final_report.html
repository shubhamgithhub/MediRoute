MediRoute

Healthcare beyond borders: from a patient's own words to the right speciality and verified Indian hospitals

Anand Chavan · Aniket More · Pravin Patil · RaviTeja Karri · Shubham Thakare

Final report · 24 November 2026

Academic project. MediRoute is a hospital discovery tool: it does not diagnose or advise treatment; a doctor at the hospital confirms the plan.

1 · Idea

Idea and use case

The problem: medical tourism to India

  • International patients (and their families) travel to India for affordable, high-quality treatment: cardiac surgery, joint replacement, transplants, cancer care, IVF.
  • Researching where to go is fragmented: hospital information is scattered, listed generically and not matched to the patient's diagnosis or report.
  • Report review is manual (a human agent reads it), and the hospital data on aggregator sites is curated by the platform, not grounded in verifiable public sources.

Users

  • Primary: international patients and caregivers researching treatment.
  • Secondary (future): hospitals receiving enquiries.

What MediRoute does

  • The patient writes the need in their own words, optionally attaches PDF reports, and picks a city or state.
  • Step 1 suggests which speciality to search for, with the evidence words and a question when unsure.
  • Step 2 lists NABH / JCI-verified hospitals for that speciality in the area, each with the reasons it was chosen.
  • Emergency and crisis messages get a fixed safety notice first.

What it does not do

  • No diagnosis, no treatment advice, no booking.
  • Scope narrowed after the mid-term review: translation, currency, PDF summary and the hospital agent were deferred to put the effort into evaluation.

Where AI is used, and how it is kept honest

WhereAI / ML usedWhyGuardrail (code)
Step 1 · evidenceLLM copies conditions, procedures, body parts; tags body areasPatients write freely (“sugar problem”, “heart is weak”); lifts lookup accuracy 87–88% → 93%Phrase kept only if ≥ 60% of its words are in the text: the AI cannot invent evidence
Step 1 · lookup voterML search: TF-IDF + MiniLM embeddings over ICD-10-CM / CCSR / CGHS, fused by rankGrounded in public medical coding data, explainableOnly the 26 fixed specialities can come out
Step 1 · LLM voterLLM picks 1 of 26 using each speciality's “route here when / not here” rulesUnderstands context the lookup missesClosed list (JSON schema); it never sees the lookup's answer (independent votes)
Step 1 · judgeLLM chooses between the two candidates, only when they disagree (~20%)Fewer questions to the patient (biggest single gain: 66% → 82% correct)Must cite a lookup evidence line and a quote from the text; asked twice in swapped order; any failure → ask
Safety netLLM quotes self-harm words the rules missedMisspellings, other languagesCan only add the crisis message, never remove one; fixed rules decide emergencies
Step 2 · rankingML search: FAISS + multi-qa-MiniLM similarityPatient wording differs from hospital wordingEligibility by verified speciality first; bonuses are visible reasons

Principle: the LLM is never trusted on its own. Every AI output is checked by code; when the checks fail, MediRoute asks the patient instead of guessing.

Step 1 metrics

  • Correct: expected speciality or an accepted alternative.
  • Confidently wrong: answered and wrong: the costly error we minimise.
  • Asks: asked the patient; correct after one answer: where the patient ends up.

Step 2 metrics (the patient sees 5)

  • P@5 (precision): share of the 5 shown that are good.
  • NDCG@5: good hospitals near the top; Hit@1: the first is good; Bad@5: an unsuitable one is shown.
  • Baselines + bootstrap 95% CI + paired permutation test.

Step 1: how the design evolved

  • Baselines first (rows 1–3): simple search looks accurate but is confidently wrong on 8–16% of cases; an LLM alone is the worst (16%).
  • Design choice: two independent voters and ask when they disagree → confident errors fall to 0–2% but too many questions (rows 5–6).
  • The guarded LLM judge and the speciality boundaries win back the answers (rows 7–8). Today, on the doctor's labels: 0 confidently wrong.

4 · Evaluation · Step 1

Step 1: real portal and unseen data

Unseen data: no sign of over-fitting

  • 110 brand-new messages, written by a model MediRoute does not use, labels fixed by construction.
  • English: 84% correct on the first message (tuned set: 85%), 1% confidently wrong, 96% correct after one answer.
  • A stronger or pricier judge (Gemini 4 Carbon, GPT-6.1 Terra) gave no gain (±3 points) at 3–6× the cost: reverted.

Where it still asks

  • Very colloquial or Hinglish wording that the medical lookup cannot match: the judge must cite a lookup line, so it asks instead of guessing.
  • Translation is out of scope, so Hinglish is excluded from the target.

4 · Evaluation

Compared with a general AI

What it shows

  • Speciality: level. A strong chatbot is as good at naming the speciality (89% vs 85%, not significant); 6 of its 7 misses send the patient to emergency care.
  • Hospitals: the clear difference is verifiability. 38% of the hospitals the chatbot names cannot be checked against any accreditation list; every MediRoute hospital is verified and comes with its reasons.

Design rules

  • Emergencies are decided by fixed rules, never by the AI; acute suicidal intent → Emergency Medicine + Tele-MANAS 14416 helpline.
  • The AI can add a crisis message, never remove one.
  • Personal details (phone, e-mail, IDs) removed before anything is sent or logged; only synthetic data in the demo.
  • Open: typed prompt injection passed 1 of 2 runs (only affects the user's own result); voter-prompt hardening planned with its own regression.

How it runs

  • One Fargate task in Sydney (ap-southeast-2) runs Nginx (HTTPS), the FastAPI / LangGraph API and the frontend together; no VM or load balancer to manage.
  • The LLM is called through OpenRouter (GPT-6 Luna); the MiniLM embedding models run inside the container on CPU (downloaded at start-up), so search needs no external service.
  • Starting a new ECS task is a deliberate manual step (auto-deploy off); the OpenRouter key is an ECS secret from AWS Parameter Store.
  • Demo uses synthetic data only; accounts live in task-local SQLite and reset when the task is replaced.

Observability

6 · Wrap-up

Limitations and next steps

Limitations

  • Ground truth: core50 is doctor-reviewed by one physician; the other test sets and the 1,381 mapping rules are team drafts.
  • 200 hospitals; speciality lists miss departments the NABH scope confirms (rare specialities P@5 0.43).
  • English only, text PDFs only (no OCR); Hinglish often ends in a question.
  • No costs, doctor profiles, availability or booking.

Next steps

  • Fill hospital speciality lists from the NABH scopes, checked by a person.
  • Doctor review of the remaining sets and the last page of the speciality list; route suicidal thoughts straight to Psychiatry (doctor's comment on C047).
  • Voter-prompt hardening against typed injection, with a regression run.
  • Admin page login and an alert rule on the CloudWatch dashboard.

Takeaway: a narrow, two-step design where AI does what it measurably improves, code checks every AI output, and every claim is benchmarked against baselines and a doctor-reviewed ground truth.
