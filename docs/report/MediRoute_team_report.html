In one page

  • What it does: a patient describes the problem in their own words; MediRoute suggests which speciality to search (Step 1) and lists verified hospitals for it in the chosen area, with the reasons (Step 2).
  • What it does not do: no diagnosis, no treatment advice, no booking. Procedures are used only when the patient wrote them.
  • How AI is used: an LLM extracts the evidence and votes; a guarded LLM judge settles disagreements. Code checks every AI output (closed lists, quotes must be in the text) and asks the patient when unsure.
  • Measured: every design change was benchmarked against baselines (keyword search, meaning search, LLM only, voting). Where AI did not beat simple rules (Step 2 capability matching), we did not use it.
  • Open: test labels and mapping rules await doctor review; hospital procedure lists are unverified; 201 hospitals only. Work after 11 Nov is local and not yet committed.

Who and why

  • Patients (including international patients) looking for treatment in India who do not know which department to approach.
  • Problem: symptoms and lay words do not map obviously to a speciality, and hospital information online is unverified and often commercial.
  • MediRoute narrows the search: one speciality to search, then hospitals whose speciality is confirmed on their own website, NABH / JCI accreditation shown.

Design rules

  • Discovery, not advice: “Suggested speciality to search”, never a diagnosis; a disclaimer on every result.
  • Ask when unsure: a short question beats a confident wrong answer; the patient's answer is used in the decision.
  • Explain every result: the evidence words for the speciality and the ranking reasons for each hospital.
  • Emergency words (chest pain with breathlessness, suicidal intent…) show an emergency notice first; routing continues.
  • Fix data at the root: no one-off override rows for single cases.

Why a portal and not a chatbot

  • Answers come only from a fixed, checked hospital list (no invented hospitals or departments).
  • Every request is logged step by step (admin page), so any answer can be audited.
  • Every stage is measured on test sets; section 9 compares a general AI on the same questions.

Why changing the judge model did not change the results

  • The judge is only called when the two voters disagree: about 20% of requests. The other ~80% never reach it.
  • Its guards require an evidence line from the medical lookup. When the lookup has no matching line (very colloquial wording), even a perfect judge cannot confirm the answer, so the app asks. The bottleneck is the evidence, not the judge.
  • Measured on 77 unseen English messages: GPT-6 Luna 84% / Gemini 4 Carbon 83% / GPT-6.1 Terra 81% first-answer correct; within 1–3 messages of each other (noise). Luna is kept: same accuracy, 3–6× cheaper, faster (section 7b).

Worked examples (real portal requests)

  • “I have kidney stones and severe pain in my lower back… Mumbai”: evidence “kidney stones”, “severe pain”, “lower back”; lookup Urology, LLM Urology → rule a: Urology (2.2 s).
  • “I am having sciatic nerve pain since 3 months”: lookup Neurology (“Lesion of sciatic nerve”), LLM Orthopaedics (“Sciatica”); rule c → the judge says unclear → one question; answer “Bones, joints and muscles” fits Orthopaedics only → rule b: Orthopaedics.

3 · Demo Q&A: why stage 3 (evidence) uses an LLM

Say this: “The evidence step turns a free-form message into checked medical phrases. It raises lookup accuracy from 87–88% to 93%, keeps the two voters independent, stops guessing on vague messages and shows the patient why, for about $0.00004 and no extra waiting time.”

#ReasonDetail / proof
1Measured accuracy gainLookup on LLM-extracted phrases 93% vs raw text 87–88% (mediroute set, Step 1 benchmark, section 7)
2Patients write freely“blocked arteries”, “sugar problem”, “heart is weak”: a fixed keyword list only finds words it already has
3Removes noise before search“my mother”, “for months”, “Mumbai”, “my child” would pull wrong matches (“child” → Paediatrics)
4Keeps the voters independentLookup searches the phrases + medical data; the LLM voter reads the whole text and never sees the lookup. Agreement can be trusted
5Checked by code, cannot inventKept only if ≥ 60% of its words are in the text; generic words and hidden identifiers dropped; every check is logged
6Safety gate for vague messagesNo medical phrase (“a good hospital in Pune”) → ask the patient, never guess
7Explains the answerKept phrases are the evidence chips, always in the patient's own words
8Helps Step 2Procedures (“endoscopy”, “knee replacement”) give the procedure bonus (+0.04 each, max 0.08)
9Almost freeRuns at the same time as the LLM voter; ≈ 258 + 35 tokens, ≈ $0.00004; temperature 0, fixed seed
10Not a single point of failureLLM down → code-only keyword matches; the lookup still runs

Likely follow-up questions

  • “Why not keywords only?” That is the no-LLM fallback. It misses wording not in the list, so more patients get a question instead of an answer. Not benchmarked on its own yet (replay test, no API cost).
  • “The LLM voter already returns evidence_terms; why a second call?” Those are the words the LLM picked to justify its own answer. Feeding them to the lookup would tie the two voters together, so a shared mistake would look like agreement.
  • “Does it slow the patient down?” No. It runs in parallel with the LLM voter; the whole of Step 1 takes ≈ 3 s.
  • “Can it hallucinate?” It can propose a phrase, but code drops any phrase that is not in the patient's text. The admin page shows every phrase as kept or dropped, with the reason.
  • “Where can I see it?” Admin page → any request → “Step 1: how the speciality was worked out”; diagram: docs/architecture/mediroute_algorithms.png.

7a · Examples: confidently wrong vs asking

  • Confidently wrong = the method gives a speciality and it is wrong, with no question and no warning. The patient goes to the wrong kind of hospital without knowing. This is the costly error.
  • Asks = the method shows the clarification form instead of guessing. It counts as “not correct” in the tables (the patient has not answered yet), but it is safe.
  • Our design trades “asks” for fewer confident errors: keyword search is confidently wrong on 14% of core50 (7 of 50); MediRoute on 0% (0) of core50 and 1% (1) of mediroute in the real portal test. The weak spot is symptom-only messages: 18 of 100 gretel messages, see below.

Labels are drafts.
  • Measures: first answer (as before); right outcome = correct, or a question on a message that is really vague; after one answer = the simulated patient answers the body-area question once.

7c · Red flags and several body areas (14 Nov)

Why this change was made

  • The 13 Nov portal test found 19 confidently wrong answers; 18 were symptom-only messages from the public gretel set. Read one by one, they were two patterns: symptoms from several body areas sent to one organ specialist (vomiting + dizziness → Gastroenterology), and red flags (chest pain with breathlessness or a racing heart, sudden loss of sight, confusion) sent to Pulmonology, Cardiology or Ophthalmology instead of emergency care.
  • Design priority agreed with the team: ask rather than guess. A question costs the patient one answer; a confident error sends them to the wrong hospital without warning.

What was changed

  • Red flags (stage 2, code): new and wider rules: chest pain (also “my chest hurts”) with breathlessness, a racing heart, dizziness or sweating; breathless with a racing heart; sudden loss of sight; new confusion. A match now answers Emergency Medicine at once, with no LLM call. Checked on every gold message: no false alarm in core50, mediroute or the 110 unseen messages.
  • Several body areas (stage 3d): the evidence LLM tags each symptom with a body area from a fixed list; code counts the areas. Neighbour pairs count as one (reflux: stomach + throat or mouth, not ear or nose; cold: nose or throat + lungs; migraine: headache + nausea). No named diagnosis or procedure and 2+ areas → ask which area troubles most; “More than one / whole body” or “Not sure” → General Medicine, with the explanation that a general physician examines first and refers.
  • Data the doctor reviews: data/processed/body_area_tags.csv, body_area_neighbours.csv, one new answer in specialty_mapping.csv. Prompt evidence_v2 (then v3: every symptom tagged; “general” only for whole-body symptoms).

How it was tested

  • The same 234 requests as section 7 (core50 50, mediroute 84, gretel 100), sent through the running app. When the app asks, a simulated patient answers the body-area question once (the area of the expected speciality, or “More than one” for General Medicine), so the right column shows where the patient ends up. This is favourable by construction: a real patient may answer less clearly.
  • Scored against two label sets. Current labels: the gold set as it is (gretel's label is the speciality of a hidden diagnosis the patient never names). Rule labels (draft, awaiting the doctor): one rule for every gretel message: red flag → Emergency; named condition → its speciality; one body area → that specialist (General Medicine also accepted); several areas or only general symptoms → General Medicine (data/evaluation/step1_gold_gretel_rule_review.csv). For the rule labels the simulated patient answers from the rule label.

8c.1 · Extraction accuracy and omission of critical details (13 Nov 2026)

What the TA asked (mid-sem feedback): “Medical information extraction should be evaluated separately for accuracy and omission of critical details.” and “Evaluate extraction errors, unsupported recommendations, and performance on ambiguous cases.” Also: “noisy medical reports”.

Why this test was done

  • Every other Step 1 test scores only the final speciality. A report can be routed correctly from one detail while others are missed, and when routing fails we could not say whether the PDF reader, the LLM or our code check lost the information.
  • The TA asked for extraction to be evaluated separately (accuracy and omissions). Missing a detail such as “CABG advised” or “carcinoma” is also the error that matters most for patients, and the procedures found here feed the Step 2 procedure bonus.

How it was done

  • Data: the 23 readable synthetic PDF reports (15 core reports + 8 layout cases: letterhead, lab table, two columns, long report, typos, personal details, two reports, report with no typed text). Unreadable files (empty, locked, scanned) are tested separately and correctly refused.
  • Labels: for each report 2–5 critical details (85 in all), tagged diagnosis / finding / procedure / body part; decisive = the details the speciality depends on (e.g. “triple vessel disease”, “CABG”). Drafted from the report texts by the team, pending doctor review (data/evaluation/report_critical_details.csv).
  • Run: each report goes through the real Step 1 pipeline with the app's model (GPT-6 Luna), as the patient page would send it (typed text + report text, cut at 6,000 characters).
  • Three levels per detail, so a loss can be located: A in the text read from the PDF (and after the cut) · B returned by the evidence LLM · C kept after the code check, i.e. used by the voters, shown to the patient and passed to Step 2.
  • Match rule: a detail counts as captured when one extracted phrase covers at least half of its words (abbreviations and close spellings count; text broken by line breaks such as “necro sis” is joined). Every miss was checked by hand.

8c.2 · Alternative ranking weights (13 Nov 2026)

What the TA asked (mid-sem feedback): “Experiments should also assess the effect of different ranking weights” and “Test alternative ranking weights rather than relying on arbitrarily assigned values.”

Why this test was done

  • Step 2 adds fixed bonuses to the similarity score (website-confirmed speciality +0.04, each matching procedure +0.04 up to 0.08, verified details +0.03, NABH +0.02, JCI +0.02, international services +0.02, evidence quality up to +0.03; total capped at 0.20). These values were set by hand.
  • The TA asked for alternative ranking weights to be tested rather than chosen arbitrarily. The question: do the bonuses help at all, and would a different choice of values give a better ranking?

How it was done

  • The real Step 2 engine was rerun on the same 45 gold requests and grades as section 8, once per weight variant; only the bonus weights changed. No LLM, no API cost; the app code was not changed.
  • 13 variants: current weights · no bonuses (similarity only) · every bonus ×0.5, ×2, ×4 · each of the 7 bonuses removed on its own (leave-one-out) · current weights searched across all of India (no area filter).
  • Scoring: NDCG@5 and Capable@3 as in section 8, with a paired permutation test against the current weights. Run twice: on grades whose website was read (as reported in section 8) and on all grades, as a robustness check. The current weights reproduce the reported NDCG@5 of 0.807 exactly.

What it shows

  • Speciality: level. On the same 100 requests (11 Nov portal test), the general AI scores slightly higher on correct answers (89% vs 85%) and asks less (4% vs 12%); it is confidently wrong more often (7% vs 3%), but 6 of its 7 misses send the patient to emergency care for chest-pain or breathing symptoms (cautious, not careless).   • Fair input: both systems got the same text: the message plus the report text for the 15 core50 report cases (a first run without the reports made the AI ask for them and was discarded).
  • Hospitals: the clear difference is verifiability. 38% of the hospitals it names cannot be checked against our NABH / JCI / website-verified list (often a local branch of a chain, or a hospital we never collected). 12 · Limitations and next steps

Limitations

  • Labels and rules are drafts: test labels, 1,381 mapping rules and body-area rules await the doctor review; four “wrong” portal cases have questionable labels.
